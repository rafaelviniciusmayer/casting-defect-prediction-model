[115] The feature engineering process expanded the feature space from 15 original process variables to 115 total features, as detailed in Section 2 of the Supplementary Materials (Table S3), creating a final dataset that incorporates domain knowledge, physical relationships, and operational metrics . This expansion facilitates model learning by explicitly encoding relationships that might be difficult for the model to discover from raw variables alone, particularly nonlinear interactions and domain-specific patterns . The expansion represents a 667% increase in the dimensionality of the input space, providing the model with a substantially richer representation of process conditions.
[116] The derived features are distributed across the following categories: 6 ratio features capturing proportional relationships between physically related variables (velocity ratio, fill time ratio, solidification ratio, pressure time ratio, temperature ratio, and sleeve aspect ratio); 3 product features representing physical quantities from multiplicative interactions (intensification energy, velocity distance, and temperature-solidification product); 2 difference features capturing gradients between related variables (temperature difference and cycle-fill difference); 30 distance features (2 per original variable) incorporating knowledge about ideal operating ranges; 45 binary range features (3 per original variable) providing categorical position information; 4 statistical aggregation features summarizing overall process state (count of variables in range, count out of range, average distance from ideal, and maximum distance from ideal); 5 domain-specific features incorporating casting-specific knowledge (sleeve volume estimate, fill volume ratio, weighted velocity, total process time, and process efficiency); and 5 mathematical transformation features capturing nonlinear relationships through logarithmic and quadratic transformations .
[117] The distance features proved particularly relevant for capturing the relationship between operating conditions and defect probability . For each process variable, the distance from center of ideal range provides a continuous measure of deviation, while the distance from range boundary indicates how far values extend beyond acceptable limits. These features enable the model to learn that values near the center of ideal ranges are associated with minimal defect risk, while values progressively farther from ideal conditions carry progressively higher risk. The binary range features complement this by providing explicit categorical signals about whether each variable is within acceptable bounds, above the ideal range, or below the ideal range, allowing the model to learn distinct patterns for different types of deviations.

[125] SMOTE application to the training data increased minority class representation substantially . For defects with at least 5 positive samples, synthetic samples were generated to achieve a 1.5:1 ratio of negative to positive samples. This process generated approximately 22,350 additional synthetic samples across all defect types, resulting in a balanced training set of approximately 42,350 samples from the original 20,000 in the development set. The synthetic samples are generated through linear interpolation in the 115-dimensional feature space, preserving the distributional characteristics of real samples while increasing representation of defect-prone conditions  .
[126] The class weights incorporated into the loss function further enhanced learning for minority classes  . Weights ranged from 1.0 (for the most frequent defects) to 10.0 (the maximum allowed, applied to the rarest defects). These weights ensure that errors in predicting rare defects contribute disproportionately to the loss function, encouraging the model to learn patterns associated with minority classes even when their representation in the training data remains limited after SMOTE application.
[127] 4.3. Model Training and Selection Results
[128] Three model architectures were evaluated using the same data pipeline, 80/20 stratified train-test split, SMOTE balancing applied to training data, and per-defect threshold optimization to maximize recall. However, as noted in Section 3.5, some methodological differences existed: cross-validation was employed only for the neural network, and cost-sensitive learning was implemented differently across architectures. Results are summarized in Table 8.
[129] The PyTorch neural network training exhibited stable convergence patterns across the 200-epoch training horizon. The class-weighted loss function, combined with batch normalization and dropout regularization, enabled effective learning despite the multi-label nature of the prediction task. Early stopping triggered at epoch 147, indicating that the model reached optimal generalization performance well before the maximum epoch limit. The learning rate scheduler reduced the learning rate three times during training as the loss plateaued, from the initial 0.0005 to approximately 0.00024 at termination. Gradient clipping prevented exploding gradients during training, with clipping events occurring primarily during early epochs when gradients were largest.
[130] XGBoost training completed in 53.67 seconds, the fastest among the three architectures despite training 28 independent binary classifiers. The gradient boosting approach demonstrated efficient learning. The model achieved particularly strong performance on frequent defects where sufficient positive examples enabled robust gradient estimation. For rare defects, the SMOTE-augmented training data provided the primary mechanism for addressing class imbalance.
[131] Random Forest training required 210.26 seconds, approximately three times longer than neural network training and four times longer than XGBoost. The training time reflects the computational cost of growing 200 trees with maximum depth 15 per defect classifier on the SMOTE-augmented training set. Despite the longer training time, Random Forest achieved the highest recall among all models (0.9838), suggesting that the ensemble of diverse decision trees effectively captures the varied patterns associated with different defect types. The balanced class weights provided by sklearn automatically adjusted for class imbalance.
[132] Stratified five-fold cross-validation was performed only for the neural network to guide early stopping and validate model stability. Cross-validation results demonstrated consistent performance across folds, with recall values ranging from 0.9621 to 0.9847 across the five stratified folds. This consistency indicates that neural network performance is robust to the specific partition of training and validation data, supporting confidence in the final test set evaluation. The standard deviation of recall across folds (0.0089) suggests stable results that would generalize to new production data with similar characteristics. Note that XGBoost and Random Forest were trained directly on the full training set without cross-validation, which represents a methodological limitation when comparing the three architectures.

The gradient boosting approach appears particularly effective at learning the complex relationships between the 115 engineered features and multi-label defect outcomes. [135] Random Forest achieved the highest recall (0.9838), detecting nearly all defects in the test set, followed by PyTorch neural network (0.9763) and XGBoost (0.9278). The recall difference between Random Forest and XGBoost (5.6 percentage points) represents a substantial practical distinction: for every 1,000 defective parts, Random Forest would miss approximately 16 while XGBoost would miss approximately 72. In industrial environments where process variables are streamed from PLC systems at cycle rates, this latency difference determines whether predictions are available in time for corrective action. Typical die casting cycle times range from 30 seconds to 2 minutes; the neural network's sub-millisecond inference ensures predictions are available essentially instantaneously relative to the production cycle.
[137] The PyTorch neural network was selected for production deployment based on the combination of high recall (0.9763), fastest inference time (0.33 ms per 100 samples), and moderate training time (70.74 s). While Random Forest achieves marginally higher recall (0.9838 vs. 0.9763), its inference latency exceeds practical real-time requirements by a factor of nearly 4,000. The neural network's sub-millisecond inference enables integration with PLC-based control systems where predictions must be available within the machine cycle time.

[163] 5. Discussion
[164] 5.1. Theoretical Implications
[165] This study contributes to the literature on defect prediction in manufacturing through several advances related to the integration of complete ML workflows and the treatment of defect prediction as an anomaly detection problem  . The positioning of quality control as anomaly detection is particularly relevant because manufacturing processes are inherently designed to produce conforming parts, making defects statistically rare events that require specialized modeling approaches. The class imbalance inherent in defect prediction, where 94.37% of parts are defect-free and only 5.63% contain defects, represents a fundamental challenge that standard classification approaches cannot adequately address without explicit countermeasures .
[166] The class imbalance problem manifests primarily through false negatives, which occur when models learn to predict the majority class (defect-free) by default, achieving high overall accuracy while failing to detect the minority class (defective parts). In manufacturing contexts, this creates a dangerous illusion of model effectiveness: a naive classifier predicting all parts as defect-free would achieve 94.37% accuracy while completely failing its intended purpose of detecting defects . The present study addresses this challenge through a dual strategy combining SMOTE oversampling and cost-sensitive learning, demonstrating that both techniques operating together achieve substantially better recall than either technique alone  . This combined approach provides a methodological template for other anomaly detection applications in manufacturing where similar imbalance patterns prevail .
[167] The first theoretical advance concerns the domain-informed feature engineering strategy, which contributes to understanding how explicit physical and operational knowledge can augment raw sensor data for defect prediction . Rather than relying solely on the model to discover relationships from 15 process variables, the creation of 100 derived features encodes established die casting physics and operational metrics directly into the representation space. This approach aligns with principles of interpretable machine learning, where model inputs reflect meaningful physical quantities rather than arbitrary transformations, facilitating both model performance and operator interpretability  .
[168] The second advance involves the integration of SMOTE oversampling with cost-sensitive learning through class-weighted loss functions, demonstrating an effective combined strategy for addressing class imbalance in multi-label defect prediction . The results indicate that using both techniques together (synthetic oversampling to increase minority class representation during training combined with weighted loss to more strongly penalize minority class errors) achieves high recall (97.63%) while maintaining acceptable precision for screening applications. This combined approach may be particularly relevant for other manufacturing quality control contexts characterized by similar imbalance patterns
[169] The third advance emerges from the comparative evaluation of neural networks, XGBoost, and Random Forest, providing evidence on trade-offs between recall, precision, inference latency, and training cost in multi-label defect prediction . The selection of the neural network for deployment based on the combination of high recall, sub-millisecond inference time, and moderate training time illustrates that architectural choice in industrial defect detection must consider not only aggregate metrics but also operational constraints such as real-time response requirements and integration with cyber-physical production systems . However, as noted in Section 3.5, the model comparison involved methodological differences: cross-validation was employed only for the neural network, and cost-sensitive learning was implemented differently across architectures. These differences represent a limitation that may have influenced the comparative results.
[170] Most significantly, the study demonstrates that the complete workflow, encompassing all stages from problem framing through implementation, yields results that isolated component studies cannot achieve (Mayer et al., 2025). Individual investigations of algorithm selection, feature engineering, or specific defect types provide valuable insights, but only comprehensive implementation across all stages produces a deployment-ready system. The workflow makes explicit the connections between stages that remain implicit in fragmented literature, providing a template for practitioners seeking to implement ML-based quality control .
[171] 5.2. Practical Implications
[172] The practical contribution of this work is the end-to-end implementation of a machine learning workflow for defect prediction in the aluminum die casting context. From data preparation through exploratory analysis, feature engineering, model training and selection, to implementation and monitoring, the methodology demonstrates how a complete ML pipeline can be applied to a real industrial casting scenario. This end-to-end approach addresses a gap in practice where prior implementations often focus on isolated stages without integrating them into a coherent workflow deployable in a specific manufacturing domain.
[173] For foundry practitioners, the study provides several directly applicable insights. The feature engineering approach, which expands 15 raw process variables to 115 domain-informed features, demonstrates how expert knowledge about ideal operating ranges and physical relationships can be systematically encoded into ML inputs. The distance features measuring deviation from ideal ranges and the binary range features indicating position relative to those ranges are particularly straightforward to implement given standard process engineering knowledge about acceptable operating conditions.
[174] A critical practical insight from this study concerns the strategic value of false positives in manufacturing quality control. The model achieves precision values around 50%, meaning that approximately half of parts flagged as potentially defective are actually defect-free. Rather than representing a limitation, this characteristic serves a deliberate operational purpose: preventing defect propagation along the value chain. In manufacturing supply chains, a defective part that escapes detection at the foundry continues through subsequent value-adding stages(machining, assembly, surface treatment, packaging, distribution)accumulating costs at each stage before eventual detection or field failure. The cost of detecting a defect at the foundry is substantially lower than detecting it after machining, and dramatically lower than a field failure that may trigger warranty claims, product recalls, or safety incidents.
[175] The asymmetric cost structure of defect detection justifies accepting false positives as an economically rational strategy. When the model predicts a part as potentially defective, the operational response is additional inspection rather than automatic scrap. Parts correctly identified as defective are removed from production before further value is added. Parts incorrectly flagged as defective (false positives) undergo additional inspection and, once verified as conforming, continue through the production process. The cost of this additional inspection is far lower than the cost of undetected defects reaching customers. The model thus functions as a screening system that concentrates inspection resources on the subset of parts most likely to contain defects, while allowing the majority of production (predicted as defect-free) to proceed without additional verification.
[176] Even with the deliberate acceptance of false positives, the screening approach substantially reduces overall inspection costs compared to 100% inspection of all production. Without predictive screening, quality assurance would require inspecting every part produced (25,000 parts in the present dataset) with associated costs for equipment utilization, operator time, and production throughput reduction. With predictive screening, only parts flagged by the model require additional inspection. The model's recall of 97.63% ensures that nearly all actual defects are captured in the flagged subset, while the flagged subset itself represents a fraction of total production. The net effect is reduced inspection volume while maintaining defect detection effectiveness.
[177] Consider the inspection economics quantitatively. If the model flags 10% of production for additional inspection while achieving 97.63% recall, inspection effort is reduced by approximately 90% compared to inspecting all parts, while still capturing nearly all defects. The 2.37% of defects missed by the model represent the residual risk, which can be further reduced through complementary quality control measures such as statistical process control monitoring, periodic audits, or downstream inspection checkpoints. The combination of predictive screening with traditional quality control creates a defense-in-depth approach where multiple layers provide redundant protection against defect escape.
[178] The threshold optimization strategy, which maximizes recall per defect type, formalizes this operational prioritization where false negatives are more costly than false positives. In manufacturing quality control, defective parts reaching downstream stages or customers represent critical failures, while false positives result only in additional inspection costs. The methodology provides a principled approach to threshold selection that can be calibrated based on specific cost structures in different manufacturing environments. Organizations with higher costs of field failure relative to inspection costs would select lower thresholds to maximize recall, while organizations with constrained inspection capacity might accept somewhat higher thresholds to reduce false positive volume.
[179] The model's inference time of 0.33 ms per 100 samples demonstrates feasibility for real-time deployment in industrial environments where predictions must be available within milliseconds of process variable measurement. This latency enables integration with PLC-based control systems where immediate corrective action is required. The explainability features that identify which variables are operating outside ideal ranges and provide specific adjustment recommendations address the practical requirement for operator trust and actionable guidance. Operators presented with both a defect prediction and a clear explanation of which process variables are contributing to risk can make informed decisions about parameter adjustments, rather than treating the model as an opaque oracle.
[180] The practical workflow demonstrated in this study also addresses the organizational challenge of ML adoption in manufacturing. Rather than presenting ML as a replacement for existing quality control practices, the methodology positions predictive models as a complement to traditional approaches. The synthetic dataset generation provides a low-risk starting point for organizations without extensive historical data archives, allowing methodology validation before committing to production data integration. The explicit workflow stages provide clear milestones for project management, enabling incremental implementation where each stage can be validated before proceeding to the next.
[181] 5.3. Limitations and Future Research
[182] Some limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, validation on real production data remains essential. The synthetic dataset, while grounded in expert knowledge, cannot fully capture the complexity of real-world variability, including sensor noise, equipment drift, batch-to-batch material variation, and rare defect modes not well-represented in the influence matrix. Future research should validate the methodology on actual production data from industrial foundries, potentially using transfer learning approaches that start from models trained on synthetic data and adapt to limited real-world labeled samples.
[183] Second, methodological differences in the model comparison may have influenced results. Stratified five-fold cross-validation was employed only for the neural network, while XGBoost and Random Forest were trained directly on the full training set. Additionally, cost-sensitive learning was implemented differently: the neural network used weighted loss functions, Random Forest used sklearn's class_weight='balanced' parameter, and XGBoost relied solely on SMOTE balancing without explicit class weights. These differences limit the comparability of results across architectures and should be addressed in future research through standardized experimental protocols.
[184] Third, the study focused on a single casting process (high-pressure die casting) and material system (aluminum alloys). The methodology could be extended to other manufacturing domains (investment casting, sand casting, forging, additive manufacturing) where similar challenges of scarce labeled data, multi-defect outcomes, and real-time prediction apply. The domain-informed feature engineering approach is generalizable, provided domain experts can articulate influence matrices and operating ranges for different processes.
[185] Fourth, the precision values around 0.50-0.54 indicate that roughly half of defect predictions are false positives. While this trade-off is acceptable when missing defects is more costly than additional inspection, future research could explore strategies to improve precision without sacrificing recall, such as ensemble methods combining high-recall and high-precision models, or hierarchical classification approaches that first screen for any defect and then classify specific defect types.
[186] Fifth, human-in-the-loop refinement of the influence matrix and ideal ranges, informed by model predictions and actual outcomes over time, could create a continuous improvement cycle where operator feedback enhances both synthetic data generation and predictive model performance. Active learning strategies that identify the most informative samples for expert annotation could further optimize this feedback loop.
[187] Finally, the study did not address temporal aspects of defect prediction, such as detecting trends toward defect-prone conditions before thresholds are crossed. Future research could incorporate time-series analysis and sequence modeling to provide even earlier warnings based on trajectory patterns in process variables.
[188] 6. Closing Remarks
[189] This study presented a comprehensive application of a machine learning workflow for predicting defects in high-pressure die casting of aluminum alloys, demonstrating the complete pipeline from data preparation through model training, evaluation, and deployment preparation. The methodology employed a synthetic dataset grounded in expert knowledge mapping, producing 25,000 samples with 15 process variables and 28 defect types that preserve physical relationships between process conditions and defect outcomes.
